How to improve your Downward Dog

A good Downward Dog is an upside-down V: hips high, spine long, arms in line with your ears, and weight shared between hands and feet. Most problems come from one of seven alignment mistakes, and each traces back to a specific tight or weak area you can train.

Good Downward Dog alignment, at a glance

  • Spine: long and flat, from hands to hips, not rounded.
  • Hips: lifted up and back, as the highest point of the pose.
  • Arms: straight, reaching in a line with your ears.
  • Legs: as straight as you can make them without rounding your back.
  • Heels: reaching toward the floor. They don't have to touch.
  • Head: hanging relaxed between your upper arms.
  • Hands: fingers spread, weight pressed through the knuckles, not the heel of the hand.

The 7 most common Downward Dog mistakes

Most of these show up best from the side, which is why they're hard to catch in a mirror. For each one: what it looks like, why it usually happens, what to do in the pose, and what to train between sessions.

01

Rounded back

What it looks like
The upper and middle back hump toward the ceiling, and the hips sit low instead of being the peak of the pose.
Why it happens
Most often tight hamstrings. When the backs of the legs can't lengthen, the pelvis tucks under and the spine rounds to make up for it. Limited awareness of how to tilt the pelvis forward makes it worse.
Fix it in the pose

Bend your knees generously, lift your hips up and back, and let your chest move toward your thighs until your back flattens. Then slowly straighten your legs only as far as your back stays long.

Exercises that help
  • Reclined hamstring stretchOn your back, draw one knee in, hold behind the thigh and straighten the leg toward the ceiling as far as is comfortable. 45 seconds each side.
  • Cat-cowOn hands and knees, alternate arching and rounding your spine with your breath. It teaches you to feel the difference between a flat and a rounded back. 8–10 slow rounds.
  • Pelvic tiltsLying on your back with knees bent, gently flatten your lower back into the floor and release. Builds control of the pelvic tilt Downward Dog depends on. 10 slow reps.
02

Knees stay bent

What it looks like
The knees stay noticeably bent even when the back is flat.
Why it happens
Tight hamstrings again. Bent knees are a sensible adjustment, not a fault, as long as you're working toward straighter legs over time. Weak quads can also make it hard to actively straighten the knee.
Fix it in the pose

Keep your hips high, then pedal one knee straight at a time, pressing the heel down and back. Firm the fronts of your thighs to help the legs straighten. Don't lock the knees hard.

Exercises that help
  • Standing forward fold, bent kneesFold forward with soft knees and let your head and arms hang. Straighten your legs gradually as the hamstrings release. 45 seconds.
  • Reclined hamstring stretchSee above. The most direct way to lengthen the backs of the legs.
  • Straight-leg raisesLying down, tighten the thigh of one straight leg and lift it to the height of the other bent knee, then lower slowly. Strengthens the quads that straighten your knees. 12 reps each side.
03

Heels far off the floor

What it looks like
You're up on the balls of your feet with your ankles barely bending.
Why it happens
Tight calves and limited ankle mobility, which is a separate limitation from tight hamstrings. Some people's heels will never reach the floor, and that's fine. The goal is to reach toward it.
Fix it in the pose

Pedal your feet, pressing one heel and then the other toward the mat. If your heels are very high, try stepping your feet a few inches closer to your hands.

Exercises that help
  • Wall calf stretchHands on a wall, step one foot well back with the heel flat and the leg straight, and lean in. 20 seconds each side.
  • Ankle mobility drillIn a staggered stance facing a wall, keep your front heel down and drive the knee forward past your toes, then return. 10 reps each side.
  • Ankle circles and point-flexSlow circles in each direction, then point and flex the foot. 8 each way, each side.
04

Weight shifted forward into the hands

What it looks like
Shoulders drift forward over the wrists, so the pose looks more like a high plank with the hips lifted, and the hands carry most of your weight.
Why it happens
Often not enough pushing strength through the shoulders and core to send the hips back. Sometimes the hands and feet are simply too far apart.
Fix it in the pose

Push the floor away with your hands and send your hips further up and back, until your weight settles into your legs.

Exercises that help
  • Plank-to-down-dog transitionsFrom plank, push your hips up and back into Downward Dog, then return to plank with control. 8 slow reps.
  • Dead bugsOn your back with knees over hips, slowly extend opposite arm and leg while keeping your lower back flat. 8 reps each side.
05

Arms not in line with the torso, or elbows bending

What it looks like
There's an angle between your arms and your back instead of one long line, your shoulders hunch toward your ears, or your elbows soften and bend under load.
Why it happens
Limited shoulder and upper-back mobility stops the arms from reaching fully overhead. Bending elbows usually points to pressing strength in the shoulders, triceps and core.
Fix it in the pose

Press firmly through your hands to straighten your arms, and reach your chest gently toward your thighs so your arms line up with your ears. Let your shoulder blades widen rather than squeeze together.

Exercises that help
  • Wall slidesBack against a wall, arms in a goalpost shape, slide your arms overhead and back down without your lower back arching. 10 reps.
  • Doorway lat stretchHold the top of a doorframe with one hand and let your hips sink away, lengthening the side of your body. 20 seconds each side.
  • Scapular push-upsIn plank with straight arms, let your shoulder blades squeeze together, then push the floor away to spread them. 12 small reps.
  • Plank holdA straight line from head to heels for 30 seconds. Builds the pressing strength that keeps your elbows straight.
06

Lifting the head to look forward

What it looks like
Your gaze goes to the front of the mat and your head breaks the line of your spine.
Why it happens
Habit, mostly, or checking on your hands. It compresses the back of the neck while you're bearing weight.
Fix it in the pose

Let your head hang between your upper arms and look back toward your knees or feet.

Exercises that help
  • Chin tucksSitting tall, draw your chin straight back to make a "double chin", hold briefly, release. 10 reps.
  • Seated neck releaseTilt one ear toward your shoulder and breathe for 20 seconds each side.
07

Wrists folding sharply and getting sore

What it looks like
Your weight sinks into the heels of your hands and the wrists bend sharply under your forearms. It's the usual cause of wrist pain in this pose.
Why it happens
Stiff wrists, plus weight drifting forward (see mistake 04) because the shoulders and core aren't taking their share.
Fix it in the pose

Spread your fingers wide and press through the base of each knuckle, especially the index finger and thumb. Lift your hips up and back to take weight out of your hands.

Exercises that help
  • Wrist mobility prepSlow fist circles in both directions, then rock gently forward and back over flat palms on hands and knees. 8 circles, 10 rocks.
  • Kneeling forearm stretchPalms down with fingers pointing toward your knees, sit back gently. Then repeat with the backs of the hands. Go lightly. 20 seconds each way.

Downward Dog FAQ

Do my heels need to touch the floor in Downward Dog?

No. Many experienced practitioners never get their heels down. Heels lifting is usually tight calves and stiff ankles, not a mistake in itself. Reach the heels toward the floor without forcing them, and prioritise a long spine.

Is it OK to bend my knees in Downward Dog?

Yes. If straight legs make your back round, bend your knees enough to lift your hips and lengthen your spine, then work on hamstring flexibility so the legs can straighten over time. A long back matters more than straight legs.

Why do my wrists hurt in Downward Dog?

Usually because too much weight is sinking into the heel of the hand. Spread your fingers wide, press through the base of each knuckle, and shift weight back toward your legs by lifting your hips up and back. Wrist mobility work before practice also helps.

How long should I hold Downward Dog?

Five to ten slow breaths is a common hold. Holding with good alignment for a shorter time is more useful than holding longer with a rounded back or collapsing shoulders.
